## 1. Source rules — personal services income of a non-resident contractor

### 1.1 A foreign resident is assessed only on Australian-source ordinary income

**KNOWN (law).** Under s 6-5(3) of the *Income Tax Assessment Act 1997* (Cth), a foreign
resident's assessable income includes (a) ordinary income derived directly or indirectly
from **all Australian sources**, and (b) other ordinary income included on some basis
other than source. By contrast s 6-5(2) assesses a resident on ordinary income from **all
sources** worldwide. The consequence: for a genuine foreign resident, ordinary income
with a **non-Australian source is not assessable in Australia** under s 6-5.
Cite: ITAA 1997 s 6-5(2)–(3). Verified 10 July 2026.
https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/itaa1997240/s6.5.html

### 1.2 Source of services income is where the services are performed

**KNOWN (law).** For income from personal exertion/services, the leading principle is
that the source is the place where the services are physically performed, not where the
contract is made or where payment is remitted.
- *Federal Commissioner of Taxation v French* (1957) 98 CLR 398: an engineer employed by
  an Australian company for work performed in New Zealand was held to have NZ-source
  income; the source of remuneration for services is where the personal exertion takes
  place. Cite verified 10 July 2026.
  https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/print?DocID=JUD/98CLR398/00004
- The principle is not absolute. Source is "a practical, hard matter of fact" (*Nathan v
  FCT* (1918) 25 CLR 183) and later cases (e.g. *FCT v Mitchum* (1965) 113 CLR 401; *FCT
  v Efstathakis* (1979) 9 ATR 867) show that where a contract is negotiated, where it is
  to be performed, and where payment is made can each carry weight depending on the facts.
  For a straightforward "turn up and do the work" services contract, *French* (place of
  performance) dominates. Verified 10 July 2026.

**INFERRED.** Theo trades as a contractor (not an employee) supplying services to two
Australian companies. The *French* line still governs the source enquiry for services
income, but the contractor/business characterisation means one should also consider
whether income has the character of business income and, if so, where the profit-yielding
activities occur — which, for a solo services provider, again points to the place the work
is done.

### 1.3 Application to Theo (hypothetical, fact-gated)

**Hypothetical A — all work performed in Thailand (ASSUMED, unconfirmed; gap G-17).**
If Theo is a foreign resident and performs 100% of the services physically in Thailand,
the ordinary/business income from HA Pty Ltd and T&H Collective would, on the *French*
principle, have a **Thai (non-Australian) source** and would **not be assessable in
Australia** under s 6-5(3) — notwithstanding that the payers are Australian companies and
pay from Australian bank accounts. This is the provisional "likely nil Australian tax"
view. It is **contingent on two unproven facts**: (i) foreign-resident status, and (ii)
that no relevant work is performed in Australia.

**Hypothetical B — some work performed while physically in Australia (the open gap).**
To the extent Theo performs any services during days physically present in Australia (e.g.
visits, meetings, work done from an Australian location), the income referable to that
work would have an **Australian source** and **would be assessable to him even as a
foreign resident** (s 6-5(3)(a)), potentially requiring an apportionment. This is the
single most important personal-level factual gap (G-17) and must be resolved with a
day-location and work-location record before any "nil" conclusion is relied upon.

## 2. Withholding obligations of the Australian payers (HA and T&H)

### 2.1 No-ABN withholding (s 12-190 Sch 1 TAA 1953)

**KNOWN (law).** A payer must withhold (currently at 47%) from a payment for a supply
made "in the course or furtherance of an **enterprise carried on in Australia** by" the
supplier, unless an exception applies or an ABN is quoted (s 12-190, Sch 1, *Taxation
Administration Act 1953*). Where the supplier is an **offshore/foreign supplier not
carrying on an enterprise in Australia**, condition (a) is not met and no-ABN withholding
does not apply; such a supplier is generally not entitled to an ABN. "Carried on in
Australia" is read by reference to the indirect tax zone (s 12-190(7)), which excludes
external territories and offshore areas. See TR 2002/9. Verified 10 July 2026.
- s 12-190: https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/taa1953269/sch1.html
- TR 2002/9: https://www8.austlii.edu.au/au/other/rulings/ato/ATOTR/2002/TR20029.html

**INFERRED.** If Theo (NZ sole trader in Thailand) is not carrying on an enterprise *in
Australia*, the companies would have **no no-ABN withholding obligation** on his invoices.
This is consistent with the offshore-services fact-pattern but depends on the same
place-of-performance facts as Part 1.

### 2.2 Foreign-resident withholding categories (Subdiv 12-FB Sch 1 TAA)

**KNOWN (law).** Subdivision 12-FB (ss 12-315, 12-317) imposes withholding on **certain
prescribed payments** to foreign residents. The prescribed categories are narrow — broadly
**works and related activities in the building/construction/infrastructure sphere,
entertainment and sports activities, and casino gaming junket activities** (prescribed by
the *Taxation Administration Regulations*; see TR 2006/12 for the works and related
activities category). General professional/consulting/contractor services performed
offshore are **not** within these prescribed categories. Verified 10 July 2026.
- Subdiv 12-FB: https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/taa1953269/sch1.html
- TR 2006/12: https://www8.austlii.edu.au/au/other/rulings/ato/ATOTR/2006/TR200612.html

**INFERRED.** On the assumed facts, Theo's contractor income does not fall within any
Subdivision 12-FB prescribed category, so **no foreign-resident withholding under 12-FB
is triggered**.

### 2.3 Ordinary PAYG (employee) withholding (s 12-35 Sch 1 TAA)

**KNOWN (law).** Salary/wage withholding under s 12-35 applies to payments to an
**individual as an employee**. It does not apply to genuine independent-contractor
payments. Whether a worker is an employee or contractor is a substance question (see the
High Court's *Personnel Contracting* [2022] HCA 1 and *Jamsek* [2022] HCA 2, which direct
attention to the written contract where the relationship is wholly in writing). Verified
10 July 2026.
https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/taa1953269/sch1.html

**GAP (G-14/G-16).** With no services agreement and no invoices on file, the
employee-vs-contractor characterisation cannot be confirmed. If the arrangement were in
substance employment, s 12-35 and the source analysis for employment income (still
*French*: place of performance) would need to be re-run. Flag for the adviser.

### 2.4 Net position on withholding (provisional)

**INFERRED.** On the assumed offshore-contractor facts, the two Australian companies
likely have **no PAYG or foreign-resident withholding obligation** on Theo's invoices.
This is contingent on (i) contractor (not employee) characterisation, and (ii) Theo not
carrying on an enterprise in Australia / performing the work offshore.

## COMPANY-LEVEL RISKS (affect Hadleigh Vernall and Henry Holmes, not only Theo)

**These risks sit at the level of the two Australian companies. They therefore affect the
co-owners — Hadleigh Vernall (50% of HA Pty Ltd) and Henry Holmes (T&H Collective) — and
the companies' own tax and compliance costs, independently of Theo's personal position.**

### C.1 Corporate residency — central management and control (CM&C)

**KNOWN (law).** A company is an Australian resident if (a) it is incorporated in
Australia, or (b) it carries on business in Australia and has either its **central
management and control (CM&C) in Australia** or its voting power controlled by Australian-
resident shareholders: definition of "resident" in s 6(1) ITAA 1936. Verified 10 July 2026.
https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/itaa1936240/s6.html

**KNOWN (companies here).** HA Pty Ltd (ABN 20 668 294 460) and T&H Collective Pty Ltd are
**Australian-incorporated**. They are therefore Australian residents under the
**incorporation limb** regardless of where CM&C sits. Their Australian residency is **not
lost** by a director managing from Thailand. Verified 10 July 2026.

**Why CM&C still matters despite incorporation:**

**KNOWN (law).** *Bywater Investments Ltd v FCT* [2016] HCA 45 confirmed that residency
by CM&C is a question of fact — where CM&C **actually abides** — determined by where the
real high-level decision-making occurs, not by the formal location of the board. Directors
who merely "rubber-stamp" decisions made by someone elsewhere do not carry CM&C to their
location. TR 2018/5 is the ATO's ruling applying this: making high-level decisions that
set the company's general policies and direction constitutes CM&C, and a company with CM&C
in a place carries on business there. Verified 10 July 2026.
- *Bywater* [2016] HCA 45: https://eresources.hcourt.gov.au/showCase/2016/HCA/45
- TR 2018/5: https://www8.austlii.edu.au/au/other/rulings/ato/ATOTR/2018/TR20185.html

**The live company-level exposure (INFERRED):** If a director (potentially Theo, if he is
a director/decision-maker of either company) **habitually exercises the company's CM&C
from Thailand**, the risk is **not** loss of Australian residency (incorporation preserves
it) but rather:

1. **Dual residency and a Thai taxing claim / permanent establishment (PE) in Thailand.**
   A company managed from Thailand may be treated by Thailand as having a PE or a Thai
   management presence, exposing company profits to Thai corporate tax and creating a
   dual-residence problem to be resolved under the Australia–Thailand double tax agreement
   (the treaty analysis is another agent's scope). This is a **cost and compliance risk
   borne by the companies and their co-owners**, not by Theo alone.
2. **Foreign-country registration, filing and payroll obligations** in Thailand triggered
   by management presence.

**What triggers it:** habitual, substantive high-level decision-making (board strategy,
major contracts, financial and policy decisions) being conducted by a director physically
in Thailand — as opposed to isolated or purely administrative acts. TR 2018/5 and PCG
2018/9 (the ATO's practical compliance guideline) set out the factors and a low-risk
"where directors' meetings and high-level decisions genuinely occur in Australia" zone.
Verified 10 July 2026.
https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?DocID=COG%2FPCG20189%2FNAT%2FATO%2F00001

### C.2 Note on proposed law — corporate residency reform (FLAGGED: not enacted)

**KNOWN (status).** In the 2020–21 Federal Budget the Government announced it would amend
the corporate residency test so a foreign-incorporated company is an Australian resident
where it has a "significant economic connection to Australia" (core commercial activities
plus CM&C in Australia). **As at 10 July 2026 this measure remains announced but
UNENACTED.** It does not change the position above and, in any event, would not assist the
*Australian-incorporated* companies here (incorporation limb already applies). Treat as a
watch-item only. Verified 10 July 2026.
https://taxboard.gov.au/consultation/corporate-tax-residency-review

### C.3 Australian PE of the companies — largely not the issue here

**INFERRED.** Because HA and T&H are Australian-incorporated residents, the "PE" concept
of concern is a **Thai** PE created by management from Thailand (C.1), not an Australian
PE. An Australian-source and Australian-residency analysis for the companies themselves is
unaffected by Theo's location; the exposure runs the other way (into Thailand).

## 3. Dividends and director's fees paid to a non-resident

### 3.1 Dividend withholding (s 128B ITAA 1936)

**KNOWN (law).** Dividends paid by an Australian resident company to a non-resident are
subject to dividend withholding tax under s 128B ITAA 1936:
- The **franked** portion of a dividend paid to a non-resident is **exempt** from dividend
  withholding tax (s 128B(3)(ga)).
- The **unfranked** portion is subject to withholding at a default **30%**, reduced by any
  applicable double tax agreement (commonly 15%, sometimes lower) — the Australia–Thailand
  treaty rate is a matter for the treaty agent.
- A non-resident gets **no franking tax offset** and **no refund** of franking credits.
- Unfranked amounts declared as **conduit foreign income** can be exempt.
Verified 10 July 2026.
- s 128B: https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/itaa1936240/s128b.html
- ATO, dividends and non-resident shareholders:
  https://www.ato.gov.au/forms-and-instructions/you-and-your-shares-2022/dividends-and-non-resident-companies-and-shareholders

**INFERRED (relevance).** If profit is extracted from HA or T&H to Theo as **dividends**
rather than contractor fees, the AU treatment flips from the s 6-5 source question to the
s 128B withholding regime. Franked dividends to Theo would generally bear **no AU
withholding**; unfranked dividends would bear withholding at the treaty-limited rate. This
is an extraction-route question flagged by gap G-16 (payment characterisation undocumented).

### 3.2 Director's fees

**KNOWN (law).** Director's fees are treated as payments for services. Source follows the
place the director's duties/services are performed (the *French* principle, Part 1.2).
Director's fees paid to a foreign-resident director are subject to **PAYG withholding**
where they are assessable in Australia; the company must withhold and report, at rates
depending on any treaty. Verified 10 July 2026.
https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/print?DocID=JUD/98CLR398/00004

**INFERRED.** If any payment to Theo is characterised as a **director's fee** for duties
performed **in Thailand**, the source is likely Thai and it is likely not assessable in
Australia (mirroring Part 1). If director's duties are performed **in Australia**, the fee
is Australian-source, assessable, and attracts PAYG withholding by the company. Again this
turns on the unresolved place-of-performance gap (G-17) and on characterisation (G-16).

## 4. Part IVA — general anti-avoidance (relevant only to any restructure)

**KNOWN (law).** Part IVA ITAA 1936 is the general anti-avoidance rule. It can apply where
(i) there is a **scheme**, (ii) a taxpayer obtains a **tax benefit** in connection with the
scheme, and (iii) having regard to the **eight factors in s 177D(2)**, it would be
concluded that a person entered into the scheme for the **sole or dominant purpose** of
enabling the taxpayer to obtain that tax benefit. The purpose test is **objective** (not
subjective motive); merely obtaining or desiring a tax benefit is not sufficient — recent
authority (*Minerva Financial Group v FCT* [2024] FCAFC 28) confirms the dominant-purpose
threshold. Verified 10 July 2026.
- Part IVA / s 177D: https://www.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/itaa1936240/
- Minerva (FCAFC): https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2024/03/tax-benefits-and-desire-lessons-from-the-minerva-part-iva-decision/

**INFERRED (application).** The current arrangement (invoicing as an offshore contractor)
is described as pre-existing, not a restructure, so Part IVA is not squarely engaged by the
status quo. **However**, any **future restructuring** designed to route income offshore or
convert assessable Australian-source income into non-assessable form — e.g. re-papering
Australian-performed work as offshore, or interposing entities to strip profit out of the
AU companies — would need to be tested against Part IVA. Per operating rule 3, no
speculative structuring is proposed here; this is a flag only.
