## 1. Section 41 Revenue Code — residence and the remittance basis

**KNOWN (enacted law).** Personal income tax on foreign-source income is governed by
**Section 41 of the Revenue Code**:

- **s.41 para 1** — Thailand-source income (from employment, business carried on, or assets
  in Thailand) is taxable whether or not paid in Thailand.
- **s.41 para 2** — a person is taxable on **foreign-source income** (income from
  employment or business carried on abroad, or from property abroad) **if that income is
  brought into Thailand**, provided that person is a **resident of Thailand** in that tax
  year.
- **s.41 para 3** — a person is a **resident of Thailand** if present in Thailand for one or
  more periods **aggregating 180 days or more in a tax (calendar) year**. Part-days of
  presence are counted (the practice is to count any day of physical presence).

The two limbs are cumulative: foreign-source income is taxed **only if** (a) the person is
resident in the year of remittance **and** (b) the income is remitted into Thailand.
Source: Revenue Code s.41, Thailand Law Library (Siam Legal) reproduction of the Revenue
Code, https://library.siam-legal.com/thai-law/revenue-code-filing-and-payment-of-tax-sections-17-27/
and the Revenue Department English Revenue Code text, https://www.rd.go.th/english/37749.html
(verified 10 July 2026).

**INFERRED (for the client).** If the brief's assertion that Theo was present ≥180 days in
Thailand in 2025 is correct, he is a **2025 Thai tax resident** and s.41 para 2 can bite on
2025 remittances of foreign-source income. The tax year is the **calendar year** (1 Jan–31
Dec), which does **not** align with either the NZ or the Australian tax year — day counts
must be re-cut on a calendar basis (gap G-03).

## 2. Departmental Instructions Por 161/2566 and Por 162/2566 — the "remit-any-year" rule

**KNOWN (enacted administrative interpretation, in force).**

- **Por 161/2566, dated 15 September 2023** (Departmental Instruction / Order of the
  Director-General). It re-interprets s.41 para 2 so that, **with effect from 1 January
  2024**, foreign-source income brought into Thailand by a Thai tax resident is assessable
  **in the tax year it is remitted, regardless of the year in which it was earned**. This
  removed the long-standing "remit in a later calendar year and escape tax" planning route.
  Sources: Mahanakorn Partners, "Comprehensive Overview of Order No. Por.161/2566 and No.
  Por.162/2566…", https://mahanakornpartners.com/comprehensive-overview-of-order-no-por-161-2566-and-no-por-162-2566-on-personal-income-tax-for-foreign-sourced-income/ ;
  KPMG GMS Flash Alert 2023-238, https://kpmg.com/xx/en/our-insights/gms-flash-alert/flash-alert-2023-238.html
  (verified 10 July 2026).

- **Por 162/2566, dated 20 November 2023.** A transitional clarification: the Por 161
  interpretation does **not** apply to foreign-source income **earned before 1 January
  2024**. Pre-2024 income remains under the old rule (only taxed if remitted in the same
  year earned), which in practice means **accumulated savings held at 31 December 2023 are
  outside the new charge** — the "savings pool" exemption. This is why a 31 Dec 2023 balance
  (gap G-06) matters: it fixes the exempt pool. Sources: Nishimura & Asahi, "The Revenue
  Department has adjusted the criteria for collecting taxes from income sources outside
  Thailand (Part 2)", https://www.nishimura.com/en/knowledge/publications/the-revenue-department-has-adjusted-the-criteria-for-collecting-taxes-from-income-sources-outside-thailand_2 ;
  Mahanakorn Partners (as above) (verified 10 July 2026).

**INFERRED (for the client).** Theo's income from HA Pty Ltd and T&H Collective for FY2024–25
and FY2025–26 is post-1-Jan-2024 income. If remitted while he is Thai-resident, it falls
squarely within the Por 161 charge in the **calendar year of remittance**. Only the portion
(if any) traceable to a **pre-2024 savings pool** would be shielded by Por 162 — and no
evidence establishes any such pool.

## 3. Current enactment status — the draft exemption is NOT law (re-verified)

**KNOWN (enacted position as at July 2026): Por 161/2566 and Por 162/2566 remain the
operative rules. No relaxation and no move to full worldwide taxation has been enacted.**

- In **June 2025** the Revenue Department floated a **draft** measure introducing a
  **two-year grace window**: foreign-source income earned from 2024 onward would be exempt
  if remitted **in the year earned or the immediately following year**. It is a *proposal*
  requiring Cabinet approval and a Royal Decree / ministerial instrument; it was **never
  enacted**. Source: Nishimura & Asahi, "Thai Revenue Department Proposes Tax Exemption for
  Foreign-Sourced Income Remittances…", https://www.nishimura.com/en/knowledge/publications/20250601-112896
  (verified 10 July 2026).

- The proposal **stalled**. Following dissolution of the House of Representatives, all
  pending legislative and Economic-Cabinet items were suspended pending formation of a new
  government; general elections were set for **8 February 2026**. Source: AIM Bangkok,
  "Thailand Foreign Income Tax Relaxation Initiative Shelved Due to 2026 Elections",
  https://aimbangkok.com/thailand-foreign-income-tax-relaxation-2025/ (verified 10 July 2026).

- Guidance current in 2026 confirms that, for the **2025 tax year filing** (the return due
  in early 2026), **existing Por 161/2566 rules remain fully in force** and there is no
  enacted exemption to rely on. Sources: HLB Thailand, "Thai Revenue Department Drafts
  Foreign Income Remittance Tax Relief", https://www.hlbthai.com/thai-revenue-department-drafts-foreign-income-remittance-tax-relief/ ;
  Forvis Mazars, "Thailand considering to ease tax rules on foreign-sourced income",
  https://www.forvismazars.com/th/en/insights/doing-business-in-thailand/tax/tax-rules-on-foreign-sourced-income
  (verified 10 July 2026).

**This independently confirms the orchestrator-verified fact:** current law = Por 161/162;
the two-year exemption = **draft only, not enacted** as of mid-2026. The adviser must
re-check status at the actual filing date, as a new government could revive or change it.

## 4. Do ATM withdrawals and foreign-card spend count as "remittance"? (Uncertain)

**KNOWN (law) / INFERRED (application) — flagged uncertain; no definitive Revenue
Department ruling.**

"Bringing into Thailand" under s.41 para 2 is not exhaustively defined for modern payment
rails. The prevailing professional view is:

- **ATM cash withdrawal in Thailand from a foreign account** — commonly treated **as a
  remittance**, because foreign funds are converted to THB and taken in-country for use.
- **Foreign debit/credit-card spend settled in THB in Thailand** — **may** be a remittance
  on the same logic; risk rises with size and frequency; a cautious approach assumes an
  auditor would deem it a remittance.
- The Revenue Department **has not issued final, binding guidance** on either method, so
  there is genuine uncertainty, and the *character* of the underlying funds (income vs
  capital vs pre-2024 pool) still governs whether any remittance is *assessable*.

Sources: Expat Tax Thailand, "How Thailand Taxes Foreign-Sourced Income (2026 update)",
https://www.expattaxthailand.com/understanding-assessable-foreign-sourced-income-in-thailand/ ;
Forvis Mazars, "Further guidance from the Revenue Department on Foreign Sourced Income",
https://www.forvismazars.com/th/en/insights/doing-business-in-thailand/tax/revenue-department-s-guidance-on-foreign-income
(verified 10 July 2026).

**INFERRED (for the client).** The brief's ~AUD 24,860 of ATM withdrawals is the most
legally exposed *and* the least substantiated item (gap G-21: the brief itself calls it
"unsubstantiated"). Whether it is (a) a remittance at all and (b) a remittance of
*assessable income* (vs capital or pool) are two separate open questions for the adviser.

## 5. Registration and filing — TIN, PND 90/91, deadlines

**KNOWN (enacted).**

- **TIN.** A person with Thai assessable income must obtain a **Taxpayer Identification
  Number** from the Revenue Department. Thai banks also request a TIN from customers who are
  Thai tax residents for CRS self-certification (see §9). Source: Revenue Department;
  Expat Tax Thailand, "Thai Banks and CRS/FATCA Requests",
  https://www.expattaxthailand.com/thai-banks-crs-fatca-compliance/ (verified 10 July 2026).
- **Return form.** Individuals with **employment income only** file **PND 91**; individuals
  with **other income** (business, service, foreign-source remittances, etc.) file **PND
  90**. On the facts Theo would file **PND 90**. Source: Gentle Law,
  https://www.gentlelawibl.com/post/thailand-personal-income-tax-2025-for-expats-and-thai-employees-pnd-90-and-pnd-91-pnd-94-payroll (verified 10 July 2026).
- **Deadline.** The annual return is due by **31 March** of the following year (paper); the
  Revenue Department grants roughly **8 extra days for online filing** (on/around 8 April).
  So the **2025** return is due **31 March 2026** (paper) / ~8 April 2026 (e-file). Source:
  PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, Thailand — Individual, https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/thailand/individual/tax-administration
  (verified 10 July 2026).

## 6. Penalties, surcharge and interest for late/non-filing

**KNOWN (enacted — Revenue Code).**

- **s.35** — failure to file **PND 90/91/94** by the due date: criminal fine of **up to
  THB 2,000**.
- **s.27** — **surcharge of 1.5% per month (or part-month)** of the tax payable, running
  from the day after the filing deadline until payment; reduced to 0.75%/month where the
  Director-General has granted a payment extension. The surcharge is capped at the amount of
  tax (i.e. it does not exceed 100% of the tax due).
- **s.22** — where the tax is **assessed** by the officer under s.20/21, a **penalty equal
  to 100%** of the tax payable may be imposed.
- **s.26** — on a s.24/25 assessment (e.g. failure to file leading to assessment), the
  penalty may be **200%** (double) of the tax payable.
- Penalties under ss.22/26 and surcharge under s.27 are "deemed tax", and the s.22/26
  penalty may be **reduced or waived** under Director-General regulations (commonly reduced
  to ~50% on voluntary disclosure / cooperation).

Sources: Revenue Code ss.22, 26, 27, 35 — Revenue Department English text,
https://www.rd.go.th/english/37745.html ; Thailand Law Library (Siam Legal), Sections
17–27, https://library.siam-legal.com/thai-law/revenue-code-filing-and-payment-of-tax-sections-17-27/
(verified 10 July 2026).

**INFERRED.** On small liabilities the fixed s.35 fine and the 1.5%/month surcharge
dominate; the percentage penalties bite mainly on an officer assessment after non-filing.

## 7. Progressive PIT rates and the core allowances/deductions

**KNOWN (enacted).** Net assessable income (after expense deduction and allowances) is
taxed on this progressive scale (unchanged for 2025/2026):

| Net income (THB) | Marginal rate |
|---|---|
| 0 – 150,000 | 0% (exempt) |
| 150,001 – 300,000 | 5% |
| 300,001 – 500,000 | 10% |
| 500,001 – 750,000 | 15% |
| 750,001 – 1,000,000 | 20% |
| 1,000,001 – 2,000,000 | 25% |
| 2,000,001 – 5,000,000 | 30% |
| Over 5,000,000 | 35% |

Source: PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, Thailand — Individual — Taxes on personal income,
https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/thailand/individual/taxes-on-personal-income ; Statrys,
Thailand Personal Income Tax Guide (2026), https://statrys.com/guides/thailand/tax-system-and-rates/personal-income-tax
(verified 10 July 2026).

**Key allowances/deductions (enacted).**

- **Personal allowance:** **THB 60,000** per taxpayer.
- **Spouse allowance:** **THB 60,000** (if the spouse has no income / files jointly).
- **Expense deduction — depends on income character under s.40:**
  - s.40(1)/(2) employment or service (hire of work): **50% of income, capped at THB
    100,000**.
  - s.40(6) specified professions / s.40(8) business/trade: higher standard deductions
    apply (e.g. **60%** for many s.40(8) activities, or actual documented expenses).

Sources: Sherrings, "Thailand Personal Tax — Deductions and Allowances",
https://sherrings.com/personal-tax-deductions-allowances-thailand.html ; Revenue Department
allowance guide, https://www.rd.go.th/fileadmin/download/english_form/220364guide-allow.pdf
(verified 10 July 2026).

**INFERRED / OPEN (material).** How Theo's NZ-sole-trader service fees are **characterised
in Thailand** — employment-like s.40(2) (50%/THB100k cap), or business income s.40(8)
(higher deduction / actual expenses) — is unresolved and **materially changes the tax**
(illustrated in §8). This is a lead adviser question (gap G-16).

## 8. Parameterised exposure model

### 8.1 The formula

Let, for a given **calendar year** in which Theo is a **Thai tax resident**:

- `R` = total **assessable** foreign-source income **remitted** into Thailand that year
  (THB). `R` **excludes** remittances of pre-2024 savings pool (Por 162), capital, and any
  amount that is not income. `R` is the quantity that **cannot be established** without a
  remittance schedule and a 31 Dec 2023 balance.
- `D` = expense deduction = min(rate × `R`, cap), where (rate, cap) = (50%, THB 100,000) for
  s.40(2) service income, or a higher figure for s.40(8) business income.
- `A` = allowances (personal THB 60,000; + spouse THB 60,000 if applicable; + others).
- **Net taxable income** `N = max(0, R − D − A)`.
- **Tax** `T = ProgressiveScale(N)` using the §7 table.
- If in future the **draft two-year exemption were enacted** (it is NOT, per §3), income
  remitted in the year earned or the next year would be exempt, so effectively `R → 0` for
  such amounts and `T = 0`.

This is a decision-support formula, not a computed liability.

## 9. CRS / automatic exchange of information exposure

**KNOWN (enacted).** Thailand implemented the **Royal Decree on Exchange of Information to
Comply with International Agreements on Taxation, B.E. 2566 (2023)** (CRS), effective
**31 March 2023**, and participates in the OECD **MCAA-CRS**. Thai financial institutions
identify account holders' tax residencies via self-certification and report reportable
accounts to the Revenue Department, which exchanges data with partner jurisdictions
(including NZ and Australia, both CRS participants). A person resident ≥180 days is a
Thai "reportable person". Sources: Revenue Department CRS Guidance,
https://www.rd.go.th/fileadmin/user_upload/FATCA_File/crs/Thailand_CRS_Guidance_280823.pdf ;
Kudun & Partners, "Navigating the Common Reporting Standard (CRS)",
https://www.kap.co.th/thought-leadership/thailands-latest-tax-revolution-navigating-the-common-reporting-standard-crs/
(verified 10 July 2026).

**INFERRED (for the client).** Theo's **NZ and Australian** account balances and income may
be reported *to* Thailand under CRS once he is (or self-certifies as) Thai tax-resident, and
Thai account data flows the other way. This increases the practical visibility of remittances
and of foreign holdings — relevant to any decision about filing. Whether NZ/AU institutions
have his Thai residency on file is unknown (no bank evidence — gaps G-05, G-08, G-13).

## 10. LTR visa pathway — foreign-income exemption (planning option, not current status)

**KNOWN (enacted).** The **Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa**, "**Work-from-Thailand
Professional**" category, carries a **statutory exemption** for foreign-source income:
**Section 5 of Royal Decree No. 743 B.E. 2565 (2022)**, with Director-General Notification
No. 427 (28 September 2022), exempts qualifying foreign-source income brought into Thailand
by an LTR holder from Thai PIT. Indicative eligibility for the Work-from-Thailand category
includes a **personal income threshold of ~USD 80,000/year** (or USD 40,000 with a master's
degree, IP ownership, or Series-A funding), employment by a qualifying **foreign** employer
(min. revenue thresholds, relaxed by BOI Announcement Por.3/2568 in Feb 2025), and health
insurance. Sources: Jus Laws, "Thailand LTR Visa for Work-from-Thailand Professionals",
https://www.juslaws.com/articles/ltr-visa-work-from-thailand-professionals ; Expat Tax
Thailand, "LTR Visa Thailand Tax", https://www.expattaxthailand.com/ltr-visa-thailand-tax-exemption-filing-guide/ ;
HLB Thailand, "Thailand Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa: Key Updates 2026",
https://www.hlbthai.com/thailand-long-term-resident-ltr-visa/ (verified 10 July 2026).

**INFERRED (for the client).** Theo is on a **DTV, not an LTR** (gap G-12), so **the Royal
Decree 743 exemption does not currently apply** — DTV holders have **no** special
foreign-income exemption and fall under the ordinary Por 161/162 regime. Whether he could
*qualify* for LTR is uncertain: his stated ~AUD 24,543 of receipts is far below the USD
80,000 (≈ AUD ~120,000) threshold, and he is a **sole trader**, not an employee of a
qualifying foreign corporate — both are likely disqualifiers on current facts. This is a
**future-planning** item, not a present shield.
